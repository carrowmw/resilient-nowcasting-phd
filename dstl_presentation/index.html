The idea

Data in, whatever its quality. Intelligence out, with an estimate of uncertainty.

When data are good, the estimate is sharp.

When data degrade, the estimate continues, and says it is less sure.

Graceful degradation is what makes an estimate safe to act on.

[1:30] This is the whole thesis in one picture. It is a schematic, not data; the real equivalent comes later. Most forecasting pipelines are built on the left: they assume complete inputs. When the sensor goes dark they either stop, or, worse, carry on producing a confident answer that is wrong. The right is what we want: the system keeps producing an estimate, the uncertainty widens as the evidence thins, and it tells the operator that a sensor is down. [Advance twice: at the second takeaway the slider drops to 55%.] This maps directly onto the MoD aim of operating in contested, degraded and denied environments. In that setting, data quality is not something you can fix upstream; it is a condition you operate in. The gold standard for operational intelligence is therefore not the most accurate model on clean data, it is the model whose confidence you can trust as conditions change. Operational intelligence

Situational awareness needs three things a sensor alone cannot give

Level 1 · Perception

What is happening at the sensors?

Raw reports, when they arrive. Gaps, dropouts and silent failures are normal.

›

Level 2 · Comprehension

What is happening everywhere, now?

Fill the silence at broken sensors; infer conditions where there is no sensor at all.

›

Level 3 · Projection

What happens next, and how sure are we?

A forecast with a calibrated range, and a flag when its inputs cannot be trusted.

A geospatial digital twin is the place these three levels meet: a live, queryable model of the physical network.

Defence relevance: decision support under denied, disrupted, intermittent and limited (DDIL) conditions; dependable, assured AI (Defence AI Strategy, 2022; JSP 936, Defence Investment Plan 2026). Urban pedestrian flow is an open, unclassified proxy with the same failure modes.

Levels after Endsley (1995), Toward a theory of situation awareness in dynamic systems, Human Factors 37(1).

[1:15] Endsley's three levels of situational awareness are a useful frame. Sensors only give you level one, and only when they are working. Level two, comprehension, means estimating the current state everywhere, including at silent sensors and at places with no sensor. Level three is projection with an honest range. The model I will describe has one output for each of those: imputation, spatial kriging and a probabilistic forecast. Pedestrian counting is the testbed because it is open, large and messy in exactly the ways a deployed defence sensor network is messy. Nothing here relies on the data being pedestrians.

Where resilient forecasting stands

The methods exist but the benchmarks are too clean

Impute, then forecast

Two separate stages. Imputation errors pass silently into the forecast.

Missingness-aware models

GRU-D, BRITS, SAITS: learn from the mask itself. Mature in healthcare.

Graph imputation and kriging

IGNNK, GRIN: borrow from neighbouring sensors. Evaluated mostly on near-complete traffic data.

Benchmark rates as reported by Cini et al., ICLR 2022 (GRIN) and Du et al., ESWA 2023 (SAITS). Newcastle rates computed from the raw archive.

[1:30] Resilient forecasting is not a new idea. Healthcare has handled heavy missingness for years, because intensive-care measurements are irregular by design; GRU-D, BRITS and SAITS come from there. Graph methods such as IGNNK and GRIN can infer unsensed locations from neighbours. The gap is in the evaluation. The standard traffic benchmarks, PEMS-BAY and METR-LA, are almost complete; missingness is usually injected at random afterwards. Our network is missing 59% of station-slots over the archive, and 28% even while sensors are installed, and those gaps are not random. That difference matters, as we will see: a method that looks robust on random masks can collapse on real outages.

Why this is difficult

A city is a complex adaptive system of systems

Coupled subsystems. Transport, retail, schools, events and weather all move pedestrians, on different clocks.

No two streets alike. Every row is a station; rhythms differ even a few hundred metres apart.

Adaptive. People reroute around closures and crowds, so behaviour shifts when conditions change.

Rare surges dominate risk. On the stadium corridor, one 15-minute slot reached 23× the usual peak.

[1:15] Each row here is one of 53 stations; each column an hour of the week; darker means busier relative to that station's own peak. The top rows have twin weekday peaks, school and commute. The bottom rows are weekend-led, retail and leisure. This heterogeneity is why a single global pattern does not work, and why borrowing information from neighbours is hard: the neighbour may obey a different rhythm. Add events, weather and people adapting to each other and you have a complex adaptive system of systems. The same is true of any human-terrain picture.

The thesis

Three experiments, each widening the question

Can a single model ingest imperfect sensor data without prior imputation, and still deliver calibrated estimates at sensors, between sensors and into the future?

Experiment 1 · one sensor

How does data quality shape error?

110 directional streams; classical and deep forecasters; gaps left as gaps.

Fragmentation, not just completeness, drives error.

›

Experiment 2 · the network

Does spatial context help?

54 stations; graph attention vs temporal-only models; 3 seeds.

Neighbours cut error 5–12%; bigger models do not.

›

Experiment 3 · resilience

Can one model do all three, under outages?

81 stations, 24 held out; RIoT (Resilient IoT) multi-task model; 103 runs.

Today's focus.

Common thread: no imputation before modelling. Gaps stay gaps; the model handles them, and error is scored only where data were observed. Critical in real-time modelling.

All three use the same Newcastle archive (2023–2026, 15-minute resolution). Each widens the scope: one stream, then the network, then the network under outages with stations held out.

[1:15] The thesis asks whether one model can take imperfect data as it arrives, with no separate imputation stage, and still produce calibrated estimates at sensors, between sensors and into the future. It builds in three steps. Experiment 1 looked at single sensors and asked how data quality shapes error. Experiment 2 asked whether spatial context helps. Experiment 3, the bulk of today, puts it together in one multi-task model and stress-tests it under outages.

Data quality

Seven ways a sensor network fails, and their defence analogues

[2:00] Each failure mode is a real snippet from the archive, paired with a defence analogue. Advance to step through. 1 Intermittent dropout: 339 thousand separate gaps; the median is one slot. Think packet loss on a contested link. 2 Zero or broken: nights go silent; we cannot tell quiet from failure. "No detection" versus "no report". 3 Fragmentation: gaps cluster; 78% chance the next slot is missing if this one is; half the missing time is in gaps longer than three hours. Like ISR revisit gaps. 4 Prolonged failure: this station was dark for 300 days. Sensor destroyed or out of supply. 5 Degraded sensor: it reports, but implausibly low. Present is not the same as trustworthy; spoofing looks like this. 6 Rare surges: the busiest fifteen minutes on Heber Street, the stadium corridor, Sunday 23 February 2025 around 16:00, consistent with a crowd leaving St James' Park (check the fixture list before claiming it). Every model under-predicts the top decile by 50 to 73 counts. The events that matter most have the least data. 7 Blind spots: 24 held-out stations where the model must infer from neighbours alone. Areas with no ISR coverage. The point: these are not edge cases to clean away. They are the operating environment.

Experiment 1 · single sensors

Patterns of missingness and their importance

Error falls steeply until contiguous runs reach about 3 hours, then flattens. kNN imputation makes the deep models worse, not better.

[1:15] Experiment 1, single sensors; this is the MDPI Sensors paper. Each dot is a directional stream; the x-axis is how long its unbroken runs of data are. Error falls steeply until runs reach about three hours, then flattens; the segmented fit beats a straight line (delta AIC 7). In a random forest on the same features, the number and length of fragments carry far more importance than overall completeness (out-of-bag R-squared 0.82). Completeness still correlates with error, so I would not say it does not matter, but structure matters more. On the right, filling the gaps with kNN before training made both deep models worse, from 56 to 66 percent CV-RMSE. That result motivates the rest of the thesis: if imputation does not help, make the model handle gaps itself.

[1:15] In Experiment 2 we widened from single sensors to the network: 54 stations across three seeds. It showed three tiers: naive baselines, temporal deep models, and spatial graph models. Adding neighbours cuts error by about 5% at 15 minutes and 10% at an hour [advance through horizons]. Crucially, more heads or layers changed nothing (within 2%): the bottleneck is network structure, not parameter size. Furthermore, single-sensor models only work when data is clean. As the table shows, real-time deployment needs five distinct capabilities: forecasting through outages, estimates at unsensed places, a calibrated range and an outage flag. To deliver all of these in a unified operational system, we built the Geospatial Digital Twin.

The model

RIoT: one network, three objectives

[1:15] Now let's look at the neural network powering what you just saw in the digital twin: RIoT. Inputs per station: counts over a three-hour window, with a unified encoding for missing values, plus weather, calendar and static context. [Advance] During training we deliberately hide blocks of data shaped like real gaps. A learned graph decides which stations inform each other, using walking distance and road type, never raw coordinates. The encoder alternates graph attention across space with a Transformer across time. [Advance] Three heads: imputation, kriging and a probabilistic forecast that also outputs an outage probability. Each maps onto a situational-awareness level.

Finding 1

Our original metric rewarded the model for predicting empty streets

Scoring the 53% of slots that were missing as zero made collapse look like accuracy.

[1:45] The first finding is uncomfortable, and I think the most valuable. On the left, the same four models under two scorecards. Under the legacy metric used in the thesis draft, which counted missing slots as zero, V4R looks best and the final model looks dreadful. Scored only on real counts, the order flips: V4R is worst. [Advance] Why? V4R had learnt to predict an empty street whenever data went missing: 79% of the time. Scoring observed targets only removes that immediately. [Advance] But the volume predicted inside gaps stays at only 13 to 21% of what it should be. It no longer predicts zero; it predicts nearly zero. Soft collapse. [Advance] Only the unified missing encoding, next slide, fixes that. The lesson for any evaluation of AI under degraded data: if the metric touches filled values, it can reward failure.

Finding 2

Make training gaps look exactly like real gaps

V5F halves outage error against V4R (31.8 → 15.8 at 3 h) and keeps 100% of the traffic volume through a 24-hour outage.

[1:30] Here we blank a quarter of sensors for increasing lengths. Left, error on those sensors; right, how much of the true traffic the model still predicts. V4R, orange, collapses to zero after an hour. [Advance] Loss hygiene, light blue, helps a little: it keeps about 13%. [Advance] Then one change: encode artificial training gaps and real gaps identically, so what the model learns from masked training data transfers to real outages. Turquoise keeps 88% of the volume. [Advance] The final model, dark blue, keeps all of it at every length and beats the seasonal baseline, dashed, by 11%. [Advance] Take unified encoding out of V5F, purple, and it collapses again once the window is fully dark. That is the single most important design decision in the thesis.

Finding 2, on real data

A real seven-hour outage, as the model saw it

[1:00] This is not synthetic. Pilgrim Street, Friday 1 May, in the test period. The sensor went offline from 09:45 to 17:15. [Advance] The final model continues along the plausible shape of a Friday, with an 80% interval. [Advance] Underneath: the share of the model's input window that is missing. From 13:00 it has no data at all. [Advance] Without unified encoding, the same architecture slides towards an empty street. Notice the edges: counts drop to 15 and 11 just before 09:45, and restart low around 17:15. That working-hours window suggests local roadworks or a contractor vehicle blocking the camera. This highlights the core operational dilemma: is the sensor blind, or is the street truly empty? Without external road-closure feeds, a naive model collapses to zero every time it loses signal—which looks right if the pavement was closed, but fails everywhere else. RIoT v5 maintains the contextual prior with calibrated uncertainty. We cannot score the gap itself, because there is no ground truth there; the synthetic outages on the previous slide are where error is formally measured.

Finding 3

Ablations reveal component contributions

Resilience rests on unified encoding and the spatial graph; the graph matters most for long outages (+47% error at 24 h without it).

[1:00] Leave-one-out from the final model: remove one component, measure the change. Blue bars mean the component helps. Unified encoding dominates: removing it raises 3-hour outage error by 82%. The learned graph is second, and its importance grows with outage length; switch the metric to 24 hours to see it. Grey bars are within noise: eight components could be dropped without measurable change, which argues for a slimmer final model. Note the trade-off on held-out stations: dropping messages from missing sensors helps outages but hurts held-out accuracy, because held-out stations depend entirely on those messages.

Finding 4

The model knows when it is blind, but its upper tail is too thin

Outage probability rises from 0.004 to 0.97 as inputs vanish (detection AUC 0.91 at 3 h, 0.999 at 24 h), and the 80% interval keeps at least nominal coverage.

[1:15] Is the uncertainty honest? Left, calibration histograms: flat would be perfect. Both models pile up on the right: the truth is often above the whole forecast distribution. V5F is flatter than V4R and its median is well calibrated (q50 coverage 0.49), but the 90th percentile covers only 82%. That is the peak problem in another form. [Advance] Right is the encouraging part. As the share of missing input grows, the outage probability rises smoothly from near zero to 0.97, and the 80% interval keeps covering at or above 80%. In operational terms: the model raises a sensor-health flag and does not become over-confident as evidence thins. That is graceful degradation, measured.

Finding 5

Where the model does not yet help

No run yet combines the best normal accuracy with the best resilience.

At held-out stations V5F is 9% worse than a neighbour profile; the best screens beat it by 8%.

[1:15] Honest limits. Each dot is a run. Left to right is normal accuracy, bottom to top is error under a 3-hour outage. The bottom-left corner is empty: E2.1 has the best normal accuracy, V5F the best resilience, and nothing has both yet. [Run context: E2.1 isolates unified missing encoding under standard i.i.d. masking—giving peak nominal accuracy, but lacking structured outage training. E3.3d adds historical seasonal-profile priors (zeroed out at held-out stations), which yields the strongest inductive transfer and lowest peak under-prediction.] [Advance] Second view: known versus held-out stations. V5F is excellent where it has trained, and worse than a simple neighbour profile where it has not. Held-out predictions collapse towards a city-wide average shape. The best held-out configurations, E2.1 and E3.3d, beat the baseline by about 8%. So inference at unsensed locations is an open problem, and I am presenting it as such.

Scalability

Data is the binding constraint, not compute

Compute is cheap. 184k parameters; 103 runs cost 277 GPU-hours. Seed replicates need about 260 more.

Data are scarce. The whole inductive test rests on 14 stations with any observed test data.

Density sweep running. At full density the model beats the baseline by 8%; across 15–45 stations single-seed transfer hovers noisily around the donor baseline. Full seeded sweep is currently running.

[1:15] Scalability. On the left, how data requirements whittle the network down: 222 candidate streams, 110 usable for single-sensor forecasting, 81 stations, 24 held out, and only 14 of those with any observed data in the test window. That is the real ceiling on what we can claim about inference at unsensed locations. [Advance] On the right, the density sweep: train on 15, 25, 35, 45 or 57 stations and test on the same 24. Summary

What we can now say

1

Missingness is signal, not noise

Encoding real and artificial gaps identically halves outage error and preserves 100% of volume through 24-hour outages.

2

The model knows when it is blind

Outage probability tracks input loss (AUC 0.91–0.999); interval coverage holds as data degrade.

3

Evaluation can reward failure

Scoring filled values made collapse look like accuracy.

4

Structure beats size

Spatial context helps (5–12%); more capacity does not. RIoT is 184k parameters.

5

Unsensed locations remain open

Held-out inference does not always beat a neighbour profile; peaks are often under-predicted.

6

Data, not compute, is the limit

Fourteen held-out stations bound every inductive claim. Scaling estimates suggest large increase in inductive performance with increased density.

Distance matters for induction.
[1:00] Six things we can now say, in order of confidence. The first three are large effects that agree between validation and test. The last three are limits that define the remaining work.

Handover

Implications for defence

Operational Principles

  • Treat missingness as signal: Exogenous gap encoding halves outage error under sustained communications denial.
  • Coupled quality telemetry: Ship an explicit outage/sensor-health probability alongside every prediction.
  • Structure beats scale for the edge: A 184k-parameter spatio-temporal graph model is deployable on constrained tactical hardware.
  • Stress tested uncertainty: Ensure prediction interval coverage remains calibrated as sensor inputs degrade, not just in nominal conditions.

Operational Boundaries

  • Spatial extrapolation is bounded: Held-out inference without local sensors does not reliably beat neighbour baselines without dense coverage.
  • Surge / peak under-prediction: Extreme anomalous spikes are damped out unless paired with explicit tactical or environment triggers.
  • Data density is the hard limit: Claims are constrained by physical station density, not model expressiveness or training compute
Four principles that transfer directly to defence sensor networks, whatever they measure. And three boundaries.
